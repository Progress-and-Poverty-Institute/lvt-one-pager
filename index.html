A primer for cities

Tax land more.Tax buildings less.

A land value tax shifts the property tax off buildings and onto the land beneath them. Same revenue, better incentives.

Today most U.S. property taxes apply one rate to a property's whole value: land and building alike. A land value tax, or split-rate tax, sets a higher rate on land and a lower rate on buildings. The rates are chosen so the city raises the same total. Each owner's bill then depends on how much of their property's value is land.

Why shift the tax?

Stop taxing what we want more of. Today, building, adding a unit, or fixing up a home raises its owner's bill. Untaxing buildings rewards that work.
Return the value the community creates. Land is valuable because of transit, schools, jobs, and neighbors nearby, not because of anything its owner did.
A tax that can't move or hide. Land can't leave town or be hidden offshore, and taxing it doesn't make less of it.
Idle land stops paying off. Holding a vacant lot or surface parking for a price rise costs more. Putting land to good use costs less.

What the research finds

Pennsylvania cities have taxed land more than buildings for decades. Studies of them find:

+14%
more new homes a month in Pittsburgh from a 5% cut in its building tax rate.1
6–8 points
faster growth in housing units per decade in two-rate towns than in similar one-rate towns.2
+1.6%
more construction value for each mill of gap between the land and building rates, across 15 two-rate and 204 one-rate towns.3
2–10 points
faster growth in housing density in the first decades after adoption, with no growth in house size.4

Effect sizes vary by study, city, and how wide the gap between the two rates is. These are effects on construction; the Bourassa figure is for Pittsburgh alone.

Who pays more? Who pays less?

We modeled a revenue-neutral shift, with land taxed at four times the building rate, for 1.9 million taxable properties in 23 U.S. cities using LVTShift, the Center for Land Economics' free, open-source toolkit.5,6

In most cities, most homes pay less, and nearly every vacant lot and parking lot pays more.

64%
of homes pay less in the typical city. Most do in 16 of 23 cities.
+131%

+114%

Rule of thumb: a property pays more only if land is a bigger share of its value than is typical in its city, as with empty lots, parking, and big lots with small buildings. Homes, where the building is most of the value, usually pay less.

Where states stand

Passed recently: Kentucky, New York, and Virginia.

Allowed for decades: Hawaii and Pennsylvania. In Pennsylvania, fewer than a dozen places still use it,8 including Harrisburg, Allentown, and Ebensburg.9,10

Bills considered: Colorado, District of Columbia, Maine, Maryland, Minnesota, New Hampshire, and Ohio.

Emerging interest: Michigan and Washington.

The map, its categories, and each state's status follow the Center for Land Economics' LVT legislation tracker, which has a page per state with the bills and current law (updated October 29, 2026).11 Abroad, Denmark, Estonia, and Australia's states tax land value today.

Doing it in your city

  1. Find the legal path. Check your state constitution's uniformity clause, and whether state law lets your city set separate land and building rates. Many cities need state legislation first.
  2. Get land values right. A split rate leans on the assessor's land values. Test them against vacant-lot sales, and fix them first.
  3. Model who wins and loses. Run a parcel-by-parcel, revenue-neutral analysis by property type and neighborhood. The Center for Land Economics' free LVTShift toolkit does this.5
  4. Check that it's fair. Compare bills by neighborhood income. Phase-ins, homestead exemptions, and deferral can ease the change.
  5. Map the landscape. Likely fans: homeowners, renters, builders, housing and transit groups. Likely skeptics: owners of vacant and underused land.

Common questions

Is it a tax increase?
No. Rates are set to raise the same total; what changes is who pays.
What about seniors and fixed incomes?
Homestead exemptions, circuit breakers, and deferral still apply under a split rate.
Does it have to happen all at once?
No. Cities can widen the gap between the two rates step by step over several years.
Won't landlords pass it on to renters?
The supply of land is fixed, so a landlord can't pass a land tax on by offering less of it; the owner bears the tax. Evidence from Denmark bears this out. When a 2007 merger of municipalities changed local land tax rates, home prices moved by the full value of the change in future tax, so the cost of using land was unchanged and the owner at the time absorbed the tax.12 The study measured home prices, not rents.
